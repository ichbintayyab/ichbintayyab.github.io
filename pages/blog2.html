Why Did I Write This? The Engineer's Existential Crisis

There is a strange moment in every developer’s life when they stare at their own code and wonder, “Why did I write this?” It usually happens after everything finally works. The bugs are gone. The console is silent. The application runs smoothly. And yet, something feels off.

“It Works on My Machine” — The Greatest Defense

Inside a developer’s brain, “it works on my machine” counts as valid evidence. It is the ultimate shield against doubt. If it runs locally, it must be correct — right? But software has a cruel sense of humor. The moment it leaves your machine, reality changes. Different environments, different dependencies, different chaos.

2 AM — When Semicolons Confess

There is something magical about 2 AM. During the day, the bug hides confidently. At night, when the world is silent and your brain is running on caffeine, the missing semicolon finally confesses. You stare at the screen for hours, only to realize the problem was a single character. Frustration turns into laughter. You question your life choices. Then you commit the fix like nothing happened.

The Inner Dialogue of an Engineer

Writing code is not just technical work; it is a psychological journey. One moment you feel like a genius architect building digital worlds. The next moment, you are googling the simplest error. Confidence and doubt exist in a constant loop. Every solved bug boosts your ego. Every new bug humbles you again.

Why We Still Love It

Despite the chaos, we continue. Because there is beauty in solving problems. There is satisfaction in watching something work because of logic you created. Even existential crises become part of the process. They remind us that software engineering is not just about code — it is about thinking, persistence, and growth.

Conclusion

So why did I write this? Maybe to remind myself that every developer experiences these moments. Maybe to laugh at the absurdity of debugging at 2 AM. Or maybe because deep down, every engineer enjoys the chaos a little bit. After all, if it worked perfectly the first time, where would the story be?
