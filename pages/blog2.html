Why Did I Write This? The Engineer's Existential Crisis

Muhammad Tayyab Saleem 2026 5 min read
💻

There is a strange moment in every developer's life when they stare at their own code and wonder, "Why did I write this?" It usually happens after everything finally works. The bugs are gone. The console is silent. The application runs smoothly. And yet, something feels off.

"It Works on My Machine" — The Greatest Defense

Inside a developer's brain, "it works on my machine" counts as valid evidence. It is the ultimate shield against doubt. If it runs locally, it must be correct — right? But software has a cruel sense of humor.

The moment your code leaves your machine, reality changes. Different environments, different dependencies, different operating systems — and suddenly the 200 lines you were proud of become a mystery box that nobody, including you, can explain.

"It worked yesterday" — said every developer, about code they wrote last week.

2 AM — When Semicolons Confess

There is something magical about 2 AM. During the day, the bug hides with confidence. At night, when the world is silent and your brain is running on caffeine and determination, the missing semicolon finally confesses.

You stare at the screen for hours, only to realize the problem was a single character. Frustration turns into laughter. You question your life choices. Then you commit the fix like nothing happened.

The Inner Dialogue of an Engineer

Writing code is not just technical work — it is a psychological journey. One moment you feel like a genius architect building digital worlds. The next moment, you're Googling the most basic syntax error.

Confidence and doubt exist in a constant loop. Every solved bug boosts your ego. Every new bug humbles you immediately. The cycle repeats, and somehow, you love it.

Why We Still Love It

Despite the chaos — the 2 AM debugging, the confusing stack traces, the mystifying bugs that fix themselves — we continue. Because there is genuine beauty in solving problems. There is deep satisfaction in watching something work because of logic you created.

Even the existential crises become part of the process. They remind us that software engineering is not just about code — it is about thinking clearly, persisting when things break, and growing through every failure.

Conclusion

So why did I write this? Maybe to remind myself that every developer experiences these moments. Maybe to laugh at the beautiful absurdity of debugging at 2 AM. Or maybe because deep down, every engineer enjoys the chaos just a little bit. After all — if it worked perfectly the first time, where would the story be?
